PRIME SUCCESSOR LAB — RESEARCH FINDINGS
Version 0.1, November 20, 2026

OUTCOME

This research iteration produced an executable, exact analytic successor
algorithm, a precision-aware variant, independent reference implementations,
a finite correctness argument, and a reproducible weighted-history experiment.
The analytic engine can compute the immediate next prime, incorporate it into
its state, and repeat. Its transition does not test prospective integers for
primality one at a time.

The outstanding objective is computational efficiency. Initialization currently
reconstructs all primes up to the starting prime, so inexpensive arbitrary
entry remains unsolved. The implementation also requires substantial numerical
precision. It is much slower than the included segmented sieve. This package makes
the proposal concrete and measurable; it does not establish a competitive new
prime generator or a new fundamental theorem about primes.

The construction does not require the Riemann Hypothesis. Its analytic family
is established research, particularly Solomon W. Golomb's 1976 paper, Formulas
for the next prime. No priority claim is made for the finite bound or its
presentation here. The sources are listed at the end of this report.

THE RESEARCH TARGET

The intended algorithm starts from a known prime and useful arithmetic state,
calculates its immediate successor, updates that state, and repeats. The
desired improvement would make initialization, updates, and exact extraction
cheaper than strong existing methods. Success must include knowing that no
intervening prime was skipped.

The starting prime already determines its successor mathematically. Historical
information may nevertheless save computation by retaining useful arithmetic
work. The question is which representation retains that work economically.
Counting the number of stored variables is insufficient: one number can carry
an enormous number of bits.

HOW THE EXACT STATE WORKS

Give every positive integer a weight that decreases as a fixed inverse power
of that integer. The zeta function represents the total of those weights.
Multiplying by an Euler factor for a prime removes the total contribution of
integers divisible by that prime. Applying the factors for every prime through
the current prime leaves the weights of integers with no such prime divisor.

The integer one remains. It contributes exactly one because it is the
multiplicative identity. Removing that explicit unit contribution leaves a
positive remainder. The smallest integer represented in that remainder is the
immediate next prime. A smaller composite would have a prime factor already
removed; a smaller prime would contradict the meaning of immediate successor.

The next prime supplies the leading contribution to the remainder. A finite
root extraction separates that leading value sufficiently to recover an exact
integer. Once recovered, one additional Euler factor removes its multiples and
restores the same state interpretation for the following step.

This is a mathematical description of the numerical state, not an instruction
to enumerate an infinite list of integers. The program evaluates the zeta
function and a finite product using enclosing arithmetic. The library's
special-function calculations and power evaluations are real computational
work, and their costs are included in the timings.

WHY FINITE EXTRACTION IS EXACT

Choose the integer exponent to be at least the unknown successor. The
remainder is greater than the inverse-power contribution of the successor
alone, because later surviving integers also contribute. For an upper bound,
allow every larger integer to contribute, including those actually removed.
An integral bound controls this enlarged tail.

Bernoulli's inequality then places the entire remainder below the inverse-power
contribution associated with one less than the successor. Taking the reciprocal
root puts the extracted real value strictly between one less than the
successor and the successor. Its ceiling is therefore exactly the successor.
The complete argument is in research/theory_review.md.

The apparent need to know the successor when choosing the exponent is resolved
by Bertrand's postulate: after a prime of at least two, the next prime is less
than twice the current prime. A sufficiently large exponent can consequently
be chosen from the current prime alone.

After the current prime reaches two, every successor is odd. The implementation
uses that fact to decode within a cell centered on an odd integer. This avoids
needing to resolve the tiny positive distance between the extracted value and
the successor. The proof gives a positive margin from the lower even boundary.
The full computed enclosure must fit within one permitted cell before any
integer is accepted.

One is supported explicitly as the initial state. It is never classified as a
prime. Starting there, the original ceiling extraction computes two from the
same weighted remainder; the code does not substitute a separately hardcoded
first answer in the analytic calculation.

REUSE AND NUMERICAL GUARANTEES

The program chooses an exponent large enough to cover several successive
primes. During that interval, it reuses the zeta value and updates the product
with one new factor per prime. When the current prime becomes too large for
that exponent, it starts a new interval and rebuilds the product at a larger
exponent. Those rebuilds are charged to advancement time.

FLINT/Arb ball arithmetic represents a real result by an enclosing interval
that includes arithmetic and approximation error. The implementation accepts
a successor only when the complete interval justifies a unique integer result.
An inconclusive enclosure triggers higher precision and a rebuild. Exceeding
an explicit resource limit raises an error instead of returning a guess.

The precision-aware variant evaluates very small correction terms with less
relative precision and decodes the final integer at a lower precision. It
still subtracts enclosing corrections from the full-precision state and retains
the same acceptance checks. It improves arithmetic effort without changing the
mathematical invariant or eliminating the expensive high-precision state.

Correctness depends on the supplied mathematical argument, complete prime
information during initialization, and the arithmetic library's correctness.
The emitted numerical records are useful diagnostics. They are not standalone
proof certificates that an independent theorem prover can replay.

MEASURED ANALYTIC PERFORMANCE

The following are medians of three repetitions, including initialization and
all requested advances. Method order rotated between repetitions. Native
process caches remained available. The bytearray sieve is the Python reference
implemented in this package, not an optimized native production library.

Starting at one and generating 200 successors, the original analytic method
took 0.054956 seconds. The precision-aware version took 0.028716 seconds, an
improvement by a factor of 1.91. The bytearray sieve took 0.000287 seconds.

Starting at 113 and generating 100 successors, the original method took
0.017185 seconds. The precision-aware version took 0.009150 seconds, an
improvement by a factor of 1.88. The bytearray sieve took 0.000180 seconds.

Starting at 997 and generating 30 successors, the original method took
0.055471 seconds. The precision-aware version took 0.026031 seconds, an
improvement by a factor of 2.13. The bytearray sieve took 0.000121 seconds.

Starting at 10007 and generating ten successors, the original method took
8.118136 seconds. The precision-aware version took 3.313460 seconds, an
improvement by a factor of 2.45. The bytearray sieve took 0.000138 seconds.
The improved analytic version was about 24000 times slower than that sieve in
this case. Small timings are environment-sensitive and should be interpreted
as measurements of this implementation, not universal constants.

The complete measurements, individual runs, source hashes, output streams,
and independent output comparisons are in results/optimized_benchmarks.json.
An earlier benchmark series, including separate block-sieve and scalar
primality-test comparisons, is in results/exact_benchmarks.json and its CSV.

WHAT THE COSTS REVEAL

At the starting prime 10007, analytic initialization constructs a prefix of
1230 primes and uses 640704 bits of working precision. The tiny remainder
contains the answer, but obtaining it from a state near one requires retaining
many digits. For this conservative implementation, working precision grows
roughly as the numerical location multiplied by its logarithm. Relative to the
number of digits in the input, this is an exponential growth problem.

Most of the largest measured runtime goes into building the Euler product.
The optimized version's median initialization was approximately 3.25 seconds;
advancing ten times after initialization took approximately 0.063 seconds.
These component medians are measured separately and need not sum exactly to
the median total. Even the subsequent advances remain expensive compared with
the sieve in this regime.

The numerical state is reusable, but this implementation also stores every
preceding prime for later exponent rebuilds. An arbitrary starting prime is
supported within declared resource limits; entering there cheaply is not
solved. The initialization explicitly generates all preceding primes instead
of treating that history as free input. Default analytic limits cap the seed
at 100000 and working precision at 8000000 bits.

The analytic wrapper records zero future-candidate primality tests in its
transitions. That accounting does not mean the library performs no work on
integers internally, nor does it imply that information has been obtained for
free. The total arithmetic time is the relevant comparison.

THE WEIGHTED-HISTORY EXPERIMENT

A separate experiment tested whether weighted arithmetic history improves
one-step prediction of the exact next-prime gap. Features included the
preceding 32 prime gaps, logarithmic normalization, uniform and exponential
weights, and weights that increase by one for each backward step. The study
also included histories over the preceding 8, 16, 32, 64, and 128 integers,
using logarithmic prime-power weights and counts of distinct prime factors.

The comparison separated a position-only predictor, a predictor using known
small-prime divisibility information, and the same divisibility information
with weighted history added. A simple arithmetic control chose the first
following number not divisible by any prime through thirteen.

The fitted models used fixed-seed tree ensembles. Their settings were selected
on an earlier validation range. Training contained 48930 examples with current
primes from 1000 to below 600000. Validation contained 14853 examples from
600000 to below 800000. The first test range contained 21763 examples from
800000 to below 1100000. A farther holdout contained 27806 examples from
1600000 through 2000000. Neither holdout supplied labels for model fitting.

Exact successor accuracy in the first test range, followed by the farther
holdout, was as follows:

Position only: 16.19 percent and 15.22 percent.
Divisibility information: 36.69 percent and 35.19 percent.
Divisibility information plus weighted history: 36.87 percent and 35.37 percent.
Simple first-survivor arithmetic control: 37.04 percent and 35.58 percent.

Weighted history added approximately 0.18 percentage points against the fitted
divisibility predictor in both ranges. It did not beat the simpler arithmetic
control on exact successor accuracy. Its mean absolute gap error was also
slightly worse than the fitted divisibility predictor, although slightly better
than the simple control. This is evidence about these features and numerical
ranges; it does not establish that every possible historical representation
fails or that the observed small gain persists at arbitrary magnitudes.

All forecasts were supplied with the true current prime and true preceding
history. These are one-step results, not repeated autonomous generation using
the predictor's own outputs. The fitted classifiers cannot predict a gap class
absent from their training data. Their unseen-class rates are recorded.

The elementary control was added after the exploratory first run to determine
whether the fitted models improved on basic arithmetic. No features, model
settings, or fits were revised after seeing the holdout scores. The study is
exploratory, not a preregistered confirmatory experiment.

An independent sieve supplied ground truth. Its setup cost and the cost of
constructing history features are recorded. A leakage check deliberately
changed future data after a chosen current prime: the next-gap label changed
while all features remained unchanged. This verifies the tested cutoff, not
an abstract guarantee about every conceivable future feature implementation.

Raw forecasts, exact-gap errors, full settings, limitations, and exploratory
paired comparisons are included in results/history_predictions.csv,
results/history_metrics.csv, and results/history_results.json. The raw files
cover 49569 held-out forecasts per method.

VALIDATION AND REPRODUCIBILITY

The final validation run passed all 26 tests with zero failures or errors.
The analytic and optimized methods were checked against independently computed
successors, including the first 200 primes and several nonzero entry points.
The suite also covers exponent changes, resource-limit refusal and recovery,
deliberately broad interval rejection, overlap between the two arithmetic
implementations, sieve boundaries, checkpoint validation, and invalid inputs.
The baseline suite includes longer streams and entry near one billion.

The executable command for the optimized method returned the expected ten
successors from 113 and passed independent trial-division verification. Hashes
of the three benchmarked implementation files match the files shipped in the
package. The validation log and machine-readable summary are in results.

The recorded exact-engine environment used Python 3.12.14 and python-flint
0.9.0 on x86-64 Linux. The history experiment used NumPy 2.3.5 and
scikit-learn 1.8.0. Dependencies and full reproduction commands are in README.md,
requirements.txt, and requirements-experiments.txt. The code, experiment
scripts, tests, proof notes, and raw outputs are included; installed third-party
libraries are not bundled.

WHAT WOULD CONSTITUTE THE NEXT ADVANCE

The first target is a representation that exposes the successor with far fewer
bits. A useful new weight system needs a mathematical extraction rule, a cheap
way to construct its state, and an economical update. Merely placing the
answer inside a high-precision constant does not reduce the work.

The second target is affordable initialization at an arbitrary large prime.
The current complete-prefix requirement must be removed or substantially
reduced. Proposed alternatives must account for their own arithmetic, storage,
and certification costs. A speedup with a fully prepared state does not by
itself solve arbitrary entry.

The third target is a new, independently evaluated family of historical
transformations. Any such experiment should retain the elementary arithmetic
control, freeze its choices before evaluating new magnitude ranges, and
distinguish isolated prediction from repeated exact generation. More weights
alone do not establish a useful recurrence.

The package now supplies an exact update rule, a finite extraction argument,
numerical checks, and measured failures of the hoped-for efficiency. The next
research question is which arithmetic representation can preserve immediate
succession while substantially reducing setup, state size, and update cost.
